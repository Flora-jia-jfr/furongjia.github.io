Aug 2024 - PresentDurham, NC

DukeNLP, Duke University

Advisor: Monica Agrawal

Understanding LLM dynamics as a function of pretraining data

We examined how evidence in pretraining corpora shapes clinical LLM behavior. We linked keyword-pair occurrences in pretraining data to model performance on clinical jargon understanding and unsupported medical claims, curated MedLingo from real-world clinical-note abbreviations, and analyzed sources of clinical information in pretraining datasets to guide future corpus design.

We tested how much clinical diagnostic benchmark performance can be explained by explicit corpus-frequency signals rather than LLM inference alone. We introduced Frequency-Based Probabilistic Ranker (FBPR), a lightweight Naive Bayes method that scores diagnoses using clinical-concept co-occurrence statistics from the same pretraining corpora as the evaluated models. FBPR achieved performance comparable to corpus-aligned LLMs, answered largely different subsets of questions, and improved performance when combined with LLM predictions through a simple hybrid method. These results show that explicit probabilistic baselines can serve as meaningful, historically grounded reference points and complementary signals for hybridization.

We studied how faithfully LLMs acquire probabilistic knowledge from pretraining text. We built a controlled continual-pretraining framework where known data-generating networks define ground-truth distributions, synthetic clinical-style corpora verbalize sampled configurations, and OLMo-2 models are evaluated by comparing native next-token probabilities with ground-truth posteriors. We found that models recover simple conditional distributions well but show scale-dependent recovery ceilings in larger settings, and that they often learn the direction of probability shifts while underestimating their magnitude.

Interpreting Dataset Shift in Clinical Notes

We addressed the need to explain, rather than only detect, distribution shifts in clinical text. We established an extensible benchmark suite that induces synthetic shifts using real clinical notes, developed methods for evaluating generated shift explanations, and introduced SIReNs, an end-to-end approach that explains distributional differences by selecting representative notes from each distribution. We are also extending this work to benchmark agent capabilities and agent harnesses for interpreting shifts in multi-factorial settings and more general textual domains.

May 2023 - June 2024Los Angeles, CA

Melady Lab, University of Southern California

Advisor: Yan Liu

Large Language Models for Time Series Forecasting

We introduced GPT4MTS, a prompt-based framework that fuses numeric time series with texts. We also built a GDELT-derived multimodal news impact dataset and showed consistent forecasting gains over strong unimodal baselines, demonstrating effective multimodal fusion and the value of extra-textual information.

We developed TEMPO, a GPT architecture-based time series framework that combines seasonal trend decomposition with adaptive, selection-based prompting for non-stationary distribution shift. It achieves zero-shot state-of-the-art across diverse and unseen (including multi-modal) benchmarks, indicating strong potential as a foundation model for time series forecasting.
